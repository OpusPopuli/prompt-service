RULE 1: SELECT A FEW AREAS — YOU ARE ALLOWED TO LEAVE THINGS OUT
This sentence is a preamble. The complete committee list is rendered
directly beneath it on the page, so you are NOT the record of what the
legislator sits on and you do not have to account for every membership.

Name the three to six subject areas that cover the most committees, in
your own words. Omitting a minor area is correct, not a loss. Trying to
represent all eighteen is what produces a list.

Do not reproduce the committee names. Name the SUBJECTS they cover.

Rewording a name is not grouping. "select committees on alternative
protein, CalFresh enrollment and ports and goods movement" is the list
again in lower case; "food policy and goods movement" is an area.

The only committees you name are the procedural ones in RULE 2.

Examples below use committees that DO NOT EXIST. They show the shape of
an answer, never its content: no committee name or policy area from an
example may appear in your output unless source_data lists it.

  - Given "Lighthouse Maintenance", "Harbour Dredging", "Kelp Forestry"
    and "Tidal Surveying", write "coastal infrastructure and marine
    resources".
  - Do not write "serves on the Committee on Lighthouse Maintenance,
    the Committee on Harbour Dredging and the Committee on Kelp
    Forestry".
  - Do not write "covers lighthouse maintenance, harbour dredging, kelp
    forestry and tidal surveying" — that is the same list, reworded.

RULE 2: NAME EVERY PROCEDURAL COMMITTEE — THIS ONE IS COMPLETENESS
Some committees exist to run the chamber, not to cover a subject:
Rules, Ethics, Legislative Audit, Governmental Organization. They have
no subject to fold into an area, so RULE 1 cannot carry them, and
dropping one means a reader cannot tell a legislator who sits on Rules
from one who does not — on the chamber's most consequential assignment.

So: scan the list for these before you write, and name every one you
find, in full and verbatim ("the Joint Committee on Rules", not
"rules"). They qualify however the name is dressed — "Rules",
"Assembly Rules", "Joint Committee on Rules" and "Committee on Rules"
are all the Rules committee, and the prefix does not excuse one.
Invent no subject matter for them. This is the one place you reproduce
a committee name, and the one rule that is about completeness rather
than brevity, so it survives RULE 1's licence to omit.

RULE 3: NO CHARACTERIZATION OF THE PERSON
Describe what the MEMBERSHIPS cover. Never infer interest, expertise,
priorities, focus, or what the person "cares about".

  - Allowed: "Quigg's memberships cover coastal infrastructure and
    marine resources."
  - Forbidden: "Quigg focuses on coastal issues."
  - Forbidden: "Quigg is a leader on marine policy."

RULE 4: NO EVALUATIVE LANGUAGE
Forbidden regardless of context: champion, advocate, focus, priority,
leader, expert, voice, passionate, effective, dedicated, tireless,
committed, strong, key, prominent, notable.

Also forbidden: progressive, conservative, moderate, liberal (exception:
if part of an official committee name in the data).

RULE 5: ONE SENTENCE
One sentence. Maximum 40 words. Over 40 means you are listing rather
than selecting: drop areas until it fits, keeping the ones that cover
the most committees and every procedural committee from RULE 2.
Present tense.

Open with the SURNAME alone, derived from the Name field: "Quigg's
memberships cover…", never the full name and never "the
representative" or "they". The sentence that follows yours also opens
with the surname, so a full name here reads as two introductions.
